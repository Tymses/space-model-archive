Guide: find a verified longest axis

The value must describe the real object's longest physical dimension—not the size of the downloaded mesh.

  1. Find the exact object.Use its official name, IAU number or designation. Check that the source refers to the same object as your 3D model.
  2. Choose a source for the object type.
    Asteroids, comets and dwarf planetsOpen the JPL Small-Body Database. Search for the object and inspect “Physical Parameters”. Prefer extent: it gives two or three body dimensions in kilometres. Use the largest listed axis.
    Planets and natural satellitesUse a NASA mission/science page or a NASA PDS shape-model record. Look for dimensions such as 140 × 120 × 100 km or three radii. Use the largest full dimension. Useful starting points: NASA Voyager fact sheet and NASA Planetary Data System.
    Scientific paper or shape modelUse the paper or dataset that actually reports the object's dimensions. Check the target, measurement method and units; link to that exact publication or dataset.
  3. Take the largest axis—not an average.For example, 40 × 20 × 16 km means enter 40 and choose km. If a source gives radii instead of full dimensions, double the largest radius. Do not mix radius and diameter.
  4. Enter the evidence.Put the largest dimension in “Verified longest axis”, select its unit, and paste the page or paper URL into “Dimension source URL”. The link should let someone check the value.
Do not use these as a verified longest axis: a single “effective”, “mean” or equivalent diameter; an orbital distance; a model-file bounding box; or a number from an unsourced third-party summary. JPL's diameter field is an effective body diameter, while extent is the field for tri-/bi-axial dimensions. If no reliable axis measurement is available, leave the field blank.

Source definitions: JPL physical-parameter fields · JPL SBDB data documentation · NASA PDS small-body shape models.

The real-world reference length is optional. Only use a value verified from a reliable source.

ABOUT SMA

A clear index for space models.

Space Model Archive organises links to original 3D model sources. The goal is to make geometry easier to discover while keeping provenance, scale, dimensions and licensing transparent.

Models remain hosted by their original providers wherever possible. SMA does not include a slicer: users prepare and slice files in their own software. Print assessments will only appear when supported by actual test data.
